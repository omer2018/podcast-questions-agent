# Interviewer playbook

How the five interviewers actually ask questions, taken from their own transcripts, and what each one would demand of a question written for an Israeli founder. The last section turns this into a scoring rubric for every questions file.

Sources for the question patterns: the full Diary of a CEO episode with Adam Neumann (September 2026, Happy Scribe transcript), the 20VC episode with Sheel Mohnot (transcript by Sajith Pai), Acquired's interview with Morris Chang (January 2025, podscripts.co) and press coverage of its interview with Jensen Huang (2023), Darknet Diaries episodes 45 and 158 (darknetdiaries.com), Jack Neel's interview with Professor Jiang (singjupost.com) and the top Shorts of all five channels.

## Steven Bartlett, The Diary of a CEO

What he is after: the person behind the result. He wants the moment that formed the guest, the price of the success, and a sentence the audience will carry home.

How he actually asks, from the Neumann episode:

1. He opens with the public record and then hands over the floor. He read Neumann the S-1 numbers (a $47B valuation, $3B in losses, the "We" trademark) and asked: "I wanted to hear directly from you, like, what happened?" When Neumann denied the trademark story, Bartlett let it go. The facts are there to show he did the work, not to win the point.
2. Within four minutes he is in childhood. He asks about Neumann's mother and then points to a childhood photo and asks what the boy was feeling.
3. He follows a belief across ages. "What did you want at 16? At 21? At 31?" The same question three times shows how the guest changed.
4. Every abstraction gets a request for an example. After "growth" he asks what drove it, then for one example.
5. He reuses the guest's own words ("paper walls", "the second before dawn") so the guest hears himself.
6. He notices energy and says so: he pointed out that Neumann's energy shifted when he talked about his three partners.
7. He invites depth and stops at the guest's line: "I invite you to go deeper", and no further push.
8. He asks about the spouse when the spouse was part of the decision (Rebecca's advice in the settlement).
9. He brings in names the audience may not know: "Who is Bill Gurley?"
10. He closes with "What's your last word?" and a list of lessons.

What he would praise: a question that only this guest can answer, built on a fact from his own public record, about a feeling or a turning point.
What he would cut: a question the guest has answered on ten other podcasts, a question that tells the guest how he felt, and any question with two questions in it.

Relevant examples for us: the Neumann opener is the template for a founder who lost a company. The childhood photo is the template for a founder whose family story is public. "What did you want at 16, 21, 31" works for any late bloomer.

## Jack Neel, Jack Neel Podcast

What he is after: the answer people are afraid to say on camera. Power, money, family, who really decides.

How he actually asks, from the Jiang transcript and his Shorts:

1. The first question is already the topic. No biography, no warm-up.
2. Questions are short and closed, so the guest has to pick a side: "Do you think there will actually be major blackouts?"
3. The follow-up is one word: "Why?", "Really?", "Okay?"
4. He restates and asks for confirmation: "So what you're saying is... is that right?"
5. He builds a hypothetical with names and a year: "Imagine Trump versus Newsom in 2028."
6. He asks for the extreme case from the guest's own field: "The wildest prenup a divorce lawyer has ever seen" (8.3M views).
7. He plays games with guests ("2 Truths and 1 Lie", 10.4M views, the most watched Short on the channel).
8. His titles are a guest descriptor plus a quoted line with an exclamation mark.

What he would praise: a short question that forces a position, and a question about how success changed the people around the guest ("His family was using him for money", 10.3M views).
What he would cut: a long set-up, a question that lets the guest answer with a lesson for founders, and anything that sounds like a press release.

Relevant examples for us: "the wildest case you've seen" fits any founder who has sat in board rooms and investor meetings. The family-and-money angle fits any founder whose wealth changed his relationships, asked through other people and never as "how much do you have".

## Harry Stebbings, 20VC

What he is after: how the decision was actually made, with numbers. He treats preparation as the product.

How he actually asks, from the Mohnot transcript and his own posts:

1. He opens on how the guest got in, and asks for it "in a very succinct two to three minutes".
2. Then advice to his past self: "What would you say to yourself on that call?"
3. Short challenges: "Do you believe that?", "Why not?", "Is there an example of that?"
4. He asks for the figure behind every story: "How big did you do for the first close?", "At what point?", "When did it get easier? Was there a tipping point?"
5. Superlatives followed by a number: "What was the single best LP...?" In the Ohanian Short: "What has been the single best investment you've ever made?" then "How much did you make?"
6. He names the uncomfortable mechanism: "Why would managers do it if you think for them it's a loss of fees?"
7. He asks about misconceptions: "What do you think are the biggest misconceptions about venture?"
8. He runs ten reference calls per guest, five with people the guest suggested and five with people he did not.
9. He tells founders that each answer will be a clip, so they should answer in self-contained sentences.

What he would praise: a question with a real number from the guest's company and a "why" behind it, and a question that sets the guest against a named peer's decision.
What he would cut: vague "tell me about your journey" questions, and questions with no number where a number exists.

Relevant examples for us: "What would you say to yourself on that call?" is exactly the shape for a founder who refused an offer. "At what point did it get easier?" is the shape for a founder who rebuilt after a collapse.

## Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal, Acquired

What they are after: the decisive moments of a company, told by the person who made them.

How they actually ask, from the Morris Chang interview and the Jensen Huang interview:

1. Every question sits on a checkable fact: a year, a product name, a revenue figure, a letter. "How did you decide to go commit so hard to 28 nanometers after having had all the problems at 40 nanometers?"
2. The set-up is a short statement and the question comes last. No compound questions.
3. They ask how and why a decision was made, rarely what happened.
4. They name the stakes: "Were there any tradeoffs where pursuing 20 nanometer and spending the billions of dollars cost TSMC in any way?"
5. They test the founder's version with the record: "But wasn't the original plan to stop building after Fab 2?"
6. They ask the founder to explain for a beginner: "So how would you explain it to a novice?"
7. They pick up the guest's own word and ask about it: "Why is it so ironic?"
8. The hypothetical that made the Jensen clip: "If you were magically 30 years old again today, and you were going to Denny's with your two best friends... what are you talking about starting?" Jensen answered "I wouldn't do it."

What they would praise: a question that names the exact moment, with its date and numbers, and asks how the decision was made.
What they would cut: a question built on a fact we haven't verified, and a question about a period with no decision in it.

Relevant examples for us: the "would you start a company again" hypothetical fits every founder who has exited, and lands hardest when the founder has already said in public that he would not.

## Jack Rhysider, Darknet Diaries

What he is after: the scene. Who was there, what happened, what was at stake.

How he actually asks, from episodes 45 and 158:

1. Questions are very short and the guest talks: "What was some of the stuff they were saying to you?"
2. He places the guest physically: "Where were you living?", "Did you discover all this in your parents' house?"
3. He checks details out loud: "Doctor Martin, was it?"
4. He reacts like the audience would: "What?", "Why would you stop? I mean, that's a pretty nice $1,000 in one night."
5. He walks through the hardest moment step by step: "You get through security just fine?", "What was that first fifteen minutes like?", "What charges did they have on you?"
6. On legal or sensitive ground he does not argue about guilt. He lets the guest tell it and adds the context in narration.
7. He shows sympathy instead of judgment: "That's gotta be a real nightmare."

What he would praise: a question that puts the guest back in one room at one moment, and a follow-up that asks for the next minute of that scene.
What he would cut: questions that ask for a lesson instead of a scene, and questions that judge.

Relevant examples for us: the step-by-step walk through the worst day fits any founder who lost a company or was pushed out of one.

## The rubric

Score every question in a questions file against these ten points. A question needs all ten to stay in.

1. Only this guest can answer it. It rests on something specific to him: a quote, a date, a number, a scene. (Bartlett, Acquired)
2. The fact behind it is verified and comes from a named source, and the guest is the person who said or did it. (Acquired, Stebbings)
3. It assumes nothing he has not said in public. (Bartlett)
4. It asks for a scene, a decision or a feeling, and never for a lesson or a "journey". (Rhysider, Bartlett)
5. It is one question, about 12 words, with at most one short sentence of context. (Stebbings, Neel)
6. A stranger understands it with no background. The context states why the detail matters. (Acquired)
7. He hasn't answered it in his previous interviews, or it goes a clear step beyond what he answered there. (Bartlett, Stebbings)
8. The answer can stand alone as a clip: it invites a first line with a number, a quote or a strong feeling. (Stebbings, Neel)
9. It is respectful: no personal wealth, and no pushing past a line the guest has drawn. (Bartlett, Rhysider)
10. Each of the five would keep it. Read it once as each of them: Bartlett asks whether it reaches the person, Neel whether it forces an answer, Stebbings whether it has the number and the why, Acquired whether it names the decisive moment, Rhysider whether it puts the guest in a room.
